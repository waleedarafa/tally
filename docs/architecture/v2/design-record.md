## 1. Business brief

Tally bills households for monthly streaming. It starts in the UAE, then Saudi Arabia and Egypt. Ten goals came out of a PALM workshop; four are high priority: G3, G4, G6 and G7.

### Business sketch

| Who pays | For what | Channels | Main costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Households in the UAE first, then Saudi Arabia and Egypt | Monthly plans (Basic, Premium), promotions, free trials | Direct, by card. Telecom partners bundling the service with mobile plans. | Content, payment fees, a small engineering team |

| G4 | Never lose or double revenue | Zero double charges. Missed or wrong charges under 0.1% of monthly revenue | Finance lead | High | Financial objectives |
| G6 | Treat customers fairly | Every charge explained. Cancel in under a minute. Under 2 billing complaints per 1,000 subscribers a month | Support lead | High | Society; quality and reputation |

### Settled conflict: G3 against G4

A known kind of offer (a discount, a trial, a fixed price) goes live in 2 working days, set up by marketing alone. A new kind of offer needs a release and finance sign-off, about two weeks. Rejected: speed always wins (risks G4), and correctness always wins (kills G3).

| Compute and storage | Everything | Commodity | Cloud utilities | High |

### What the map reveals

- **Efficiency gap.** Building any of the twelve product or commodity components is an efficiency gap. For a real company, the map's signal is: buy most of billing.
- **Instability.** Invoicing must be stable (G4), yet it depends on price calculation, which depends on offer experiments, the most volatile component (G3). Step 6 designs the protective boundary.
- **Drift.** Billing vendors will add offer experiments to their products. When they do, our one custom activity becomes a product. Revisit then.
- **To check.** Access granting sits left because of our own platform and telecom partners. Step 3 shows whether it carries real rules or only plumbing.

### G10, own billing

No decision yet; Step 5 decides build or buy. Options on record: buy hosted billing and build offer experiments on top; run an open-source platform and extend it; build it all. The map's signal for a real company is one of the first two. This course builds, accepting the efficiency gap for learning, and keeps product-stage parts simple.

## 3. Discover

Three stories now run end to end: renewal, upgrade and failed payment. The big picture covers all six needs from Step 2 with 13 events and 5 pivotal events. This step names signals only; Step 4 draws boundaries.

### Pivotal events

Offer published · Subscription started · Invoice issued · Subscription cancelled · Month closed. Payment captured was judged the end of the invoice's story, not a new phase.

### The three stories

| Story | Events, in order | Numbers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renewal | Renewal came due → Amount for the period worked out → VAT calculated (tax service) → **Invoice issued** → Payment requested → Payment captured (provider) → Invoice paid → Receipt sent (email) → Next period started | 20.00 + 1.00 VAT = 21.00 AED |
| Upgrade | Upgrade requested → **Plan changed** → Access upgraded at once → Proration worked out → Adjustment invoice issued → Payment requested → Payment captured → Invoice paid | Basic 12 to Premium 20 on day 16 of 30: credit 6.00, charge 10.00, net 4.00 + 0.20 VAT |
| Failed payment | **Invoice issued** → Payment requested → Payment declined → Retry scheduled (day 3) → “Update your card” sent → Retry declined → **Account marked overdue** (day 7) → Final notice sent → **Access suspended** (day 10) → Subscriber called support. Then either: card updated → payment captured → account cleared → access restored; or debt written off (day 30) → **Subscription cancelled** | 21.00 AED; day counts assumed |

A new actor appeared in the failed payment story: the collections policy, owned by finance.

### Domain story: failed payment, told by support

1. Subscriber calls support agent: “I can't watch.”
2. Support agent opens the account history.
3. Support agent finds an unpaid invoice and two declined payments.
4. Support agent sends a payment link to the subscriber.
5. Subscriber updates the card on the provider's page.
6. Billing run retries the payment and restores access.

Sentence 3 shows support needs invoices, payments and access state in one view.

### Boundary signals

| Signal | Where | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Handover | Marketing publishes an offer; the billing run uses it | Offer work and billing work differ in owner and pace |
| Handover | Payment goes out to the provider and comes back | Money movement sits apart, behind an external system |
| Handover | After the second decline, the collections policy takes over | Recovering money follows finance's rules |
| Name change | Offer → amount for the period → invoice line → payment | Four meanings of the same money |
| Name change | Customer (marketing), account (finance), subscriber (support) | One thing or three |
| Disagreement | “Suspended”: subscription or only access? | Being subscribed and being able to watch may be separate facts |

## 6. Connect

The context map has seven contexts, one read model and five external systems, with no dependency cycles. Account became the seventh context. Every relationship names its pattern and its reason.

### Account decision

Assumed fact from finance: one account can pay for several subscriptions. Options: inside Invoicing (rejected: Subscription would read from Invoicing while Invoicing listens to Subscription, a cycle); inside Subscription (rejected: an account outlives and spans subscriptions); **a small Account context upstream of all (chosen)**. Names settled: account is who pays; subscriber is who watches; customer is marketing's segment word.

### Message flows

| Story | Messages | Key choices |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renewal | 9 | Invoicing asks Offers and Pricing for the amount (PriceQuote); Payment collects by opaque reference R-55 |
| Upgrade | 8 | Proration is pricing arithmetic, answered by Offers and Pricing; Access reacts to “plan changed” |
| Failed payment | 10 | Collections commands retries through Invoicing; sets account standing through Account; cancels subscriptions by command after write-off |

### Cycle found and fixed

First draft: Collections published “billing blocked” and Invoicing listened, while Collections listened to Invoicing. Fix: Collections sets the account's standing in Account; Invoicing and Access read it. Write-off cancels subscriptions by command, so Subscription never listens to Collections.

### Relationships

| Upstream | Downstream | Pattern | What crosses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Offers and Pricing | Subscription, Invoicing | OHS + PL | PriceQuote: amount lines, currency, period, reason |
| Account | All contexts | CF | Account ID, currency, billing day, time zone, saved method, standing |
| Account | Collections | OHS | Set standing: blocked, cleared |
| Subscription | Access, Invoicing | PL | Started, plan changed, period renewed, cancelled |
| Subscription | Collections | OHS | Cancel an account's subscriptions |
| Payment | Invoicing | OHS + PL | Collect by reference; succeeded or failed by reference |
| Invoicing | Collections | PL + OHS | Invoice issued, payment failed, paid; request payment again |
| Card provider | Payment | ACL | Provider calls, translated |
| Tax service | Invoicing | ACL | VAT for an address and amount |
| Telecom partners | Access | ACL | Bundle activated, ended |
| Invoicing, Access, Collections, Account | Support view (read model) | CF | Published events |
| Email service, ledger | Invoicing, Collections | CF | Their APIs and formats |
| Money | All contexts | SK | Amount, currency, rounding |

Dependency order, upstream first: Account and Payment; Offers and Pricing; Subscription; Access and Invoicing; Collections; support view. No team patterns yet: one team (C3).

### Rules

- **R1.** Payment knows nothing about invoices; it collects against an opaque reference.
- **R2.** Invoicing receives amounts; it never applies offer rules.
- **R3.** Only Collections sets an account's standing; everyone else reads it from Account.
- **R4.** Offers and Pricing exposes only PriceQuote.
- **R5.** Money holds only amount, currency and rounding.

Done-when check: passed. Standing values are minimal (good, blocked); Step 7 completes them with finance. Kill Bill check, after the map: Kill Bill has a separate account module, attaches blocking states to accounts, and pays by payment reference; our map reached the same shapes independently.

### Invariant test

Hardest candidate: "a cancelled subscription is never invoiced again". It needs only Subscription: Invoicing invoices a period only after Subscription announces it, and Subscription never renews a cancelled subscription. Invoicing's own rule (one invoice per subscription per period) guards against duplicates. A renewal and a cancellation at the same instant is a runtime ordering risk for Step 13, not a boundary problem.

### Offers and Pricing

**Purpose.** Lets marketing launch offers fast, and answers one question for everyone else: what does this subscription owe for this period, and why?

**Classification.** Core · Revenue · Custom-built · owner: Marketing

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Offer | a sellable plan with its phases and terms |
| Kind of offer | discount, free trial, fixed-price phase, bundle price; anything else is new |
| Phase | a stretch of time with one price, such as “first month free” |
| PriceQuote | the answer: amount lines, currency, period, reason |

**Rules that must always hold**

1. A new kind of offer cannot be published without finance sign-off. Known kinds need none (Step 1).
2. A published offer never changes for people already on it. A change makes a new version.
3. The same subscription, period and offer version always give the same PriceQuote.
4. Proration is by days: unused days are credited, remaining days are charged at the new price.
5. On upgrade, a promotion does not carry over unless the offer says so (H5).

| Direction | Kind | Message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In | Query | What does S owe for this period? (Invoicing) |
| In | Query | Is this plan offered to this account? (Subscription) |
| In | Query | What does this change cost? (Invoicing) |
| Out | Query | Account currency (Account) |

**Assumption.** A1: new kinds of offer, launched fast, win subscribers.

### Subscription

**Purpose.** Keeps track of what each customer signed up for, over time: trial, plan, periods, cancellation.

**Classification.** Supporting · Engagement · Product · owner: Product

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Subscription | one plan bought by one account |
| Period | the stretch of time one invoice covers |
| Plan change | an upgrade or downgrade, with its effective date |
| Cancellation | an end date, always a period end |

**Rules that must always hold**

1. A subscription has exactly one current plan and one current period.
2. Periods never overlap and leave no gaps.
3. An upgrade takes effect at once. A downgrade takes effect at period end (H6).
4. A cancellation takes effect at period end. There is no refund for the current period (H11).
5. Periods follow the account's billing day.

| Direction | Kind | Message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In | Command | Start, change plan, cancel (subscriber app) |
| In | Command | Cancel an account's subscriptions (Collections) |
| Out | Event | Started, plan changed, period renewed, cancelled |
| Out | Query | Is this plan offered? (Offers and Pricing) |
| Out | Query | Billing day (Account) |

**Assumption.** One account can hold several subscriptions.

### Access

**Purpose.** Decides whether a person can watch right now, whatever the reason they are allowed to.

**Classification.** Supporting · Engagement · Custom-built · owner: Product

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Subscriber | the person who watches |
| Source of access | a subscription, a trial or a partner bundle |
| Suspended | viewing stopped; billing facts unchanged |

**Rules that must always hold**

1. A subscriber can watch if at least one source is active.
2. A source billed by Tally does not count while its account is blocked.
3. A trial gives access from sign-up, before any payment, for the length the offer sets (H7).
4. During payment retries, access continues until the account is blocked on day 10 (H7).
5. A partner bundle gives access without any Tally subscription.

| --- | --- | --- |
| In | Event | Started, plan changed, cancelled (Subscription) |
| In | Event | Standing changed (Account) |
| In | Event | Bundle activated, ended (partners, through a translation layer) |

**Assumption.** Partners bill their own bundle customers (H2).

### Invoicing

**Purpose.** Turns amounts into correct invoices, and tracks what is owed and what is paid.

**Classification.** Generic · Compliance · Product · owner: Finance

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Invoice | a legal record of what an account owes for a period |
| Line | one amount from a PriceQuote |
| Credit note | the only way to correct an issued invoice |
| Reference | the opaque label Payment collects against |

**Rules that must always hold**

1. An issued invoice never changes. Corrections are credit notes (G7).
2. There is at most one invoice per subscription per period (G4).
3. Invoicing receives amounts. It never applies offer rules (R2).
4. The VAT point is the invoice date. The account's time zone decides what “due” means (H8).
5. An upgrade is charged at once, on an adjustment invoice (H5).
6. No new invoice is issued while the account is blocked.

| Direction | Kind | Message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In | Event | Period renewed, plan changed (Subscription) |
| In | Event | Payment succeeded or failed (Payment) |
| In | Command | Request payment again (Collections) |
| Out | Event | Invoice issued, payment failed, paid |
| Out | Command | Collect by reference (Payment) |
| Out | Query | PriceQuote (Offers and Pricing) |
| Out | Query | Bill-to and standing (Account) |
| Out | Query | VAT (tax service, through a translation layer) |

**Assumption.** A bought invoicing product could replace this context, because it only receives amounts.

### Collections

**Purpose.** Recovers money when a payment fails, by following finance's schedule.

**Classification.** Supporting · Revenue · Product · owner: Finance

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Schedule | finance's steps: retry, notify, block, write off |
| Overdue | unpaid after the second retry |
| Write-off | debt given up; the account is closed |

**Rules that must always hold**

1. Retry on day 3 and day 7. Overdue on day 7. Block on day 10. Write off on day 30 (H4).
2. Collections never moves money. It asks Invoicing to request payment again.

## 9. Style

Tally has two architecture quanta. Billing runs as a modular monolith with one database. Access runs as a separate service with its own copy. The decision is ADR-001.

### Question 1: how many quanta?

| Shape | Qualities (Step 8) | Tied to others by a waiting call? | Quantum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Money path: Subscription, Invoicing, Payment | Correctness, recoverability (D1–D3) | Yes. Renewal, invoice and collect request must not be lost between steps | Billing |
| Collections, Account | Medium on everything | Yes. Everyone asks Account; Collections commands three contexts | Billing |
| Core: Offers and Pricing | Deployability (D4, D5) | Yes. Invoicing waits for a PriceQuote on every renewal | Billing |
| Access | Availability (D13) | No. It needs only a copy, which events can bring | Access |

The core stays in billing. Splitting it out would not shrink the damage of a bad release, because Invoicing waits on it for every renewal. It would only add a network hop. D5 asks for weeks, not hours, so a safe pipeline and a feature flag meet it.

### Candidates against the drivers

| Driver | A One modular monolith | B Monolith + Access apart (chosen) | C Service-based | D Microservices | E Event-driven |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D1 renewal exactly once | easy | easy | work | hard | hard |
| D3 nothing lost in a crash | easy | easy | work | hard | work |
| D5 new offer kind in 2 weeks | work | work | easy | easy | hard |
| D13 watch while billing is down | fails | easy | work | easy | easy |
| D10 test a month in a minute | easy | easy | work | hard | hard |
| D7 trace a charge in a day | easy | easy | easy | work | work |
| G9, C3 run by about six people | easy | easy | work | hard | hard |
| Quanta | 1 | 2 | 1 (shared DB) | 7 | 1 to many |

D2 (never charged twice) and D6 (14 renewals a second) do not decide: every style faces the same work for them.

### ADR-001. Billing as a modular monolith; Access as a separate quantum

**Status.** Accepted. Evaluated again in Step 15.

**Context.** Seven bounded contexts (Step 7). Top three: correctness, recoverability, deployability; auditability and privacy as a constraint (Step 8). About 14 renewals a second on the busiest day (D6). One team of about six (C3, G9). Java and Spring Boot (C1).

**Decision.** Offers and Pricing, Subscription, Invoicing, Collections, Payment and Account, plus the support view, run as one deployable unit with one database. Each context is a module with its own schema; no module reads another's tables. Access runs as a separate service with its own copy of who may watch, fed by asynchronous events. Inside billing, calls are synchronous and in process. Asynchronous messaging is used only between the two quanta.

**Consequences.**

- Good: a renewal, its invoice and its collect request can share one database transaction (D1, D3).
- Good: one unit to run; a month of billing testable in one process with a fake clock (G9, D10).
- Good: watching survives billing outages and deploys (D13). Audit trails stay in one database (D7).
- Cost: Offers and Pricing cannot deploy alone, so deploys must be routine and safe (Step 14).
- Cost: when billing is down, sign-ups and plan changes stop; watching does not.
- Cost: module boundaries hold only while the build checks them (Step 10).
- Cost: one broker to run, and events to Access must never be lost (Step 13).

**Compliance.** The build fails if a module uses another module's internal classes or tables (Step 10). A test stops billing and checks that Access still answers (D13). D1 and D3 tests run with two copies and a forced crash.

**Alternatives rejected.**

- A, one modular monolith: Access shares billing's process and database, so D13 fails.
- C, service-based: seven deployables for six people, network hops on the money path, and the shared database still ties Access to billing.
- D, microservices: the money path becomes sagas across databases (D1, D3); too much to run for six people (G9).
- E, event-driven: only eventual consistency on the money path (D1); flows hard to test (D10).
- Offers and Pricing as its own quantum: adds a network hop to every renewal without shrinking the damage of a bad release.

**Revisit when.** A second team owns Offers and Pricing with its own release rhythm (Step 11). Finance buys an invoicing product (Step 5). D5 tightens from weeks to days.

**Notes.** Spring Modulith can check module rules and store events in the same transaction as the change. Steps 10 and 13 decide whether to use it.

Done-when check: passed. Every part of the decision names a driver and the contexts it affects. D13's 99.9% figure is assumed; if product says watching may stop with billing, option A becomes the choice. Kill Bill check, after the decision: Kill Bill is one Java server with internal modules sharing one database and a persistent event bus, with several copies allowed, which matches Quantum 1. It sends push notifications so other systems keep their own copy, which matches the Access split. Nothing changes.

## 10. Modules

Each context in the billing unit is one module: a small public API and hidden internals. Downstream modules depend on upstream modules' APIs, so code dependencies point the opposite way to the context map's arrows. Seven tests fail the build when a rule breaks.

### Allowed dependencies

| Module | May use | Ce | Ca | Instability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| account | money | 1 | 5 | 0.17 |
| payment | money only (R1) | 1 | 1 | 0.50 |
| offers | money, account (read) | 2 | 2 | 0.50 |

Deferred: one build module per context, compiler-enforced; revisit when a second team arrives (Step 11) or a module is about to be extracted.

## 12. Aggregates

Full aggregate design for Offers and Pricing, Subscription, Invoicing and Payment. Collections, Account, the support view and Access stay simple records with a version column. The renewal runs as four transactions, each changing one aggregate, linked by events: Subscription, Invoice, Payment, Invoice.

### Size decisions

- Subscription holds only the current period and its number, not the full history. S2 (no gaps or overlaps) then holds by construction, and renew(n) plus a version check makes D1 safe with two copies running. Rejected: full period history, which grows for ever and is already in events and invoices.
- One aggregate per invoice. Rejected: one statement aggregate per account, because every subscription on an account renews on the same billing day (S5), so invoices would collide exactly at peak. The account balance is a read model.
- One Offer aggregate, with published versions as value objects inside it. Rejected: Offer and OfferVersion as separate aggregates, because publishing would then change two aggregates.

### Aggregates

| Aggregate | Context | Enforces | Commands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Offer | Offers and Pricing | O1 to O5 | Edit draft, publish, withdraw |
| OfferKind | Offers and Pricing | only finance approves a new kind; approval never withdrawn | Register, sign off |
| Subscription | Subscription | S1 to S5, D1 | Start, change plan, cancel, renew(n), cancel for write-off |
| Invoice | Invoicing | I1 to I5; I2 through its ID (subscription + period) | Issue, issue adjustment, record payment result |
| CreditNote | Invoicing | corrections never edit an invoice; total credited never exceeds the invoice (assumed) | Issue credit note |
| Payment | Payment | P1 to P4, D2; ID = the reference | Collect, record provider result, resolve unknown |

### Findings

- I6 (no new invoice while blocked) is reclassified as a policy. Blocking lives in Account, so no Invoicing aggregate can enforce it. A race is corrected by a credit note.
- Renewal against cancellation at the same instant (open since Step 7): the version check orders them; a cancel requested before period end but processed after renewal triggers "renewal reversed" and a credit note.
- Write-off becomes one cancel command per subscription, each its own transaction.
- Invoice numbers: gap-free sequences (assumed legal requirement; finance confirms) need one counter per country. Chosen: take the number in the same transaction as the invoice, a stated exception to one-aggregate-per-transaction. At about 5 ms per lock (assumed), a counter serves about 200 invoices a second against a peak of 14. Revisit with a numbering worker if load grows tenfold; use a plain database sequence if gaps are allowed.

Done-when check: passed, with the one stated exception. Every Step 7 invariant for these four contexts is held by one aggregate or reclassified (I6), and every command changes one aggregate. Kill Bill check, after the design: Kill Bill invoices per account per billing run and serialises invoice and payment work with an account-level lock, the per-account size we rejected. It fits because its invoices are per account; ours are per subscription (I2). If finance wants one invoice per account, the size decision reopens. Nothing changes now.

## 15. Evaluate

A lightweight architecture evaluation (SAIP chapter 21). Sixteen scenarios walked through ADR-001 to ADR-011: the ten high-value scenarios from the Step 8 utility tree, and six from a played brainstorm (S1 to S6). Six brainstormed scenarios produced four risks, itself a signal that the played stakeholders missed the unhappy paths.

## Slice 1R. What building taught us

Slice 1R reshaped the first run's code to this design. It ran as seventeen pull requests, #13 to #29, in the `tally` repository. Every acceptance test from AT-1 to AT-17 passes. The module graph now equals the target graph of Step 10, with one temporary edge from Access to the events of Subscription and Account. The full account is `docs/briefs/slice-1r-report.md`.

The design held. No context moved, no aggregate split, and no style decision changed. What changed was smaller and sharper: eight places where the design assumed something the code proved false. Each one is below, with what it changed.

### What the code proved wrong

| # | The design assumed | What happened | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A provider can report a request's status by its key (Step 13) | Spike S-2: no provider has that lookup. Repeating the request with the same key is the only way to ask, and after 24 hours the provider forgets the key, so a repeat charges again | ADR-026: look up by reference, then repeat with the key only inside the key's lifetime, then never repeat |
| 2 | A requested or sent attempt that is too old is unknown (ADR-018) | The provider is called only after Sent is committed. So a Requested attempt was never sent, whatever its age. | 3 | A reference lookup tells us about the attempt we are checking | The lookup shows every attempt's charges. | 4 | A false decline is corrected later | The day-3 retry would charge a falsely declined card a second time before any correction | ADR-026: Payment looks the reference up before every retry. A charge found means no new attempt |
| 5 | "Add fields only" makes an event change safe (ADR-016) | Jackson 3 refuses an event stored without a new primitive field, so every older event would fail to read | Rule: an added event field is a nullable reference type. Test FF-13 enforces it |

| 7 | A duplicate is any clash on insert | A clash on a different constraint was read as "already done". A renewal was lost, and no health metric showed it | Rule 11: a duplicate guard names the fact it guards. Any other failure is a failure |

## Slice 2, step 0. Tests that cannot disturb each other

Slice 1R ended with four flaky tests: `InconsistentLookupIT`, `ReconciliationIT`, AT-15 and AT-12. Each was fixed by a local patch, and each patch worked. But every new test that waited on background work would have needed to know about the patches. So Slice 2 started with a spike, before any new feature.

### What the spike found

The first guess was wrong. I had said several cached test contexts ran their job runners at the same time, each on its own clock. They did not: the context cache holds one context, and Spring closes it before loading the next. The real hazards were three. A context left rows rescheduled on its own clock, and a later context met them on a different clock. A context's own scheduled job raced its test's direct calls. And test-started instances shared the container's database.

Four options were measured, ten runs each where it mattered.

| Option | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Status quo, with the patches | Green, but 12 failures per run with the patches removed |
| One shared test clock (my candidate) | Worse: failed in 3 of 3 runs, because every row an earlier test left behind became live work for every later test |
| One database per test context | Isolated contexts, but 9 times a test class reused the previous class's database |
| **One database per test class, copied from a template migrated once per run** | Every patch removable. About 55 ms per copy, 4 s per suite run |

The rollout's own ten runs then found the hazard the spike had only inferred: `ReconciliationIT` failed 3 times in 10, because its own scheduled reconciliation ran between two of the test's direct calls. A setting for that class fixed it, and the next ten runs were clean.

### Decision record

| ADR | Decision | Checked by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 029 Test isolation | **Accepted.** No test class meets a row it did not create. Every test class, and every set of instances a test starts by hand, gets its own PostgreSQL database copied from a template migrated once per run. A test that needs instances competing for rows starts them on one database it owns (AT-9, AT-10). A test that waits on a periodic job moves its clock to the job's next run; no test rewrites a job or queue row to make work due. A test that needs a queue fault asks `platform` for it. Drivers: D10, and a suite whose failures mean something | F13 (no test outside `platform` writes a due time or status; no exceptions); `PerContextDatabase` |

The cost is accidental realism: a test no longer stumbles on rows other tests left behind. No test asserted on that. Real competition between instances (ADR-003) stays tested wherever a test builds it on purpose.

### A production defect the spike found

`QueuePoller.stop()` shut its workers down before the poll in progress ended. A row claimed in that moment was rejected, and its worker permit was never returned. A one-worker queue, which every job queue is, then claimed nothing after a restart. Fixed in its own pull request, with a test that reproduces the restart.

Done-when check: passed. The decision names its evidence (ten-run sets, before and after), and its rule is checked by a test, not remembered. Kill Bill check: its database-backed test base, [`GuicyKillbillTestSuiteWithEmbeddedDB`](https://github.com/killbill/killbill/blob/3868a5b2339c3b86531904d6738a36e1e82933bf/util/src/test/java/org/killbill/billing/GuicyKillbillTestSuiteWithEmbeddedDB.java), starts one embedded database for the whole suite and cleans every table before each test method (`cleanupAllTables` in a `@BeforeMethod`). Both isolate tests by their data, as 029 does. Kill Bill wipes one shared database before every method; Tally gives every test class a fresh copy. Kill Bill's is finer-grained, per test method; ours is per test class, so rows a class creates stay visible to its later methods. Nothing changes.

## Decision from step 0: ADR-031

Tally's schedule for checking an unknown payment could not keep D2's promise. D2 says an unknown outcome is resolved within 1 hour. `ReconciliationSchedule` checked 5 minutes after the attempt became unknown, then 1 hour after that check, then daily.

A payment times out during a short provider blip:

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | The charge times out. The attempt is unknown |
| 5 min | First check. The provider still struggles; the lookup fails |
| 60 min | D2's limit passes. `PaymentUnknownTooLong` pages on-call |
| 65 min | Second check. It resolves the payment, 5 minutes after the page |
| 65 min to 25 h | No check at all |

Two things follow:

1. **One failed check breaks D2.** After that, the system itself cannot resolve the payment within the hour. Moving the alert earlier would only give a person the schedule's job.
2. **Repeating with the key (026 step 2) gets two chances, not twenty hours.** The key window is 20 hours, but the third check comes at about 25 hours. So any payment the first two checks miss goes to a person through `PaymentUnknownPastKeyWindow`.

Amends: 026's schedule, and the first run's reconciliation record (FR-018), where the schedule came from. Confirms: engineering for B or C; the business only for A. Needed before: production, because D2 is a promise to customers. Evidence came first: a test showed the gap before any fix.

**Evidence (#52).** `UnknownCheckScheduleIT` follows an unknown attempt whose checks change nothing. It sees checks at 5 and 65 minutes, the attempt still unknown at 60, and the next check due at 25 hours 5 minutes, after the key window. A deliberate change to the schedule made the test fail with the new times, so it can catch a change. The fake provider cannot fail a lookup (CONTRACT.md A14), so the test reaches "checked, nothing changed" another way: the lookup shows nothing and the key repeat is refused.

**Decision: B, chosen by Waleed and accepted on 13 October, as built in #52.** Checked by `UnknownCheckScheduleIT` (the observed check times, against the real database), `UnknownCheckScheduleTest` (late checks, a short key window) and the rule tests. Its scope:

- It applies to unknown attempts. The check times count from when the attempt became unknown, not from the last check, so delays do not add up.
- The repeat with the key runs at every check inside the 20-hour window, as 026 allows.
- Declined attempts keep today's schedule. A false decline is caught by the lookup before every retry (026), not by D2's hour.
- `PaymentUnknownTooLong` moves to 50 minutes.

One limit no option removes: in a real outage the provider's lookup goes down too, and no schedule resolves a payment the provider cannot be asked about. B resolves it within an hour of recovery. Option C (a check when the breaker closes) would make that minutes; it waits until a real outage shows the hour is too slow.

**Consequences, as built.** An unresolved attempt gets 24 lookups and up to 23 key repeats in its first day, up from 2 and 2. Reconciliation takes at most 100 attempts a minute, about 3.3 provider requests a second, inside the 5 a second left under the provider's limit. So checks start running late above about 250 new unresolved unknowns an hour; the old schedule managed about 3,000. That limit binds only while the provider cannot answer, when a late check costs nothing because it would fail anyway. After recovery the backlog drains at 100 a minute, and most attempts resolve at their first check. So after a long outage, a payment is resolved within about an hour plus the backlog from recovery. If that proves too slow, option C is the next step.
